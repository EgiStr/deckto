# Arc Tags — giving the storyline a shape

Tag every slide with exactly one arc role. The tags come from Nancy Duarte's
*Resonate* arc, adapted for decks. `[BOOK/STANDARD]` Duarte, *Resonate* —
`paywalled — book`; mechanics verified via `[C] [open]`
https://www.duarte.com/blog/move-presentation-audience-with-story-techniques-in-presentations

## The four tags

| Tag | Meaning | Typical slides |
|---|---|---|
| `what-is` | Current reality the audience already agrees with | context, problem, status quo, the honest "here's where we are" |
| `what-could-be` | The change you're proposing — creates tension against `what-is` | solution, approach, the shift |
| `call-to-action` | Explicitly what you want the audience to do | the ask, the decision requested |
| `new-bliss` | The world with the idea adopted — the closing image | vision, close |

## Rules

1. **Open on `what-is`.** Establish agreed reality first; without it the proposal has
   no contrast to push against.
2. **Close on `new-bliss`, not on the ask.** The last slide is the world after
   adoption. A deck that ends on a funding request ends on the presenter's need, not
   the audience's future.
3. **Alternate for tension.** `what-is` → `what-could-be` pairs are what hold
   attention; a long run of one tag flattens the deck.
4. **Every `what-is` tension needs a `what-could-be` answer** somewhere after it.
   Unresolved problem slides leave the audience anxious, not persuaded.
5. **`call-to-action` appears once** (twice only if there's a genuine mid-deck gate).

## Failure patterns to catch in Phase 2

- **All `what-is`** — a status report, not a pitch. Rule 1 fails.
- **All `what-could-be`** — no shared ground; audience has nothing to attach the idea to.
- **Ends on `call-to-action`** — common and costly; the deck closes on your need.
- **`new-bliss` before the ask** — the vision already landed, the ask feels like an afterthought.
